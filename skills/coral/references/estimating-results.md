# Estimating results without inventing them

Estimate only when the person requests help or a meaningful measured result is unavailable. The goal is a **defensible approximation**, not a more impressive story.

1. **Separate the claim.** What is known directly? What outcome is only suspected? What period, population, baseline, or counterfactual is missing?
2. **Select the right model.** Prefer direct records; otherwise use simple transparent arithmetic with inputs the person knows or can reasonably bound. Common forms:
   - Time saved = (previous time per unit − new time per unit) × affected units.
   - Relative change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100%, provided the baseline is not zero.
   - Avoid claiming business revenue or productivity gain from technical speed alone without evidence connecting them.
3. **Expose assumptions.** Label each input as measured, recalled, estimated, or unknown; give units and time window. Ask for missing high-impact inputs instead of guessing them.
4. **Use a range where warranted.** Calculate low/high scenarios from stated assumptions. Don't imply precision greater than the inputs. Never turn a guess into a decimal-heavy exact figure.
5. **Check attribution and causality.** If many factors could have changed, describe an association or contribution rather than claiming single-handed causation.
6. **Get confirmation.** Present the calculation, its limits, and the proposed wording. Do not present it as a verified result unless the person confirms genuine supporting evidence.
7. **Keep outputs synchronized.** If both STAR and CORAL are generated, use the same units, timeframe, assumption status, qualifiers, and attribution in both.

Wording: "estimated", "approximately", "based on [stated assumptions]", or "projected" for a forecast; **"measured"** only when measured. A theoretical upper bound must be labeled as such. For a resume, if the caveat makes a numerical claim misleading, prefer a truthful qualitative result.

**Never:** invent baselines; assume usage volume; make up customer or employer data; round an uncertain estimate into an assertive number; claim a goal was met when it was not measured; or calculate a percent improvement with incompatible units.
